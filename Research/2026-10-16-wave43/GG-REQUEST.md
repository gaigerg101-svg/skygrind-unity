# GG's request for wave 43 (2026-10-16, after playing n42-r4 up to level 3), verbatim

> I want you to treat everything below as a comprehensive set of feedback and direction for the next major iteration of the game. Do not simplify, ignore, or cut any of these ideas. I want you to research the best solutions, look at successful games in this genre—especially ULTRAKILL, other boomer shooters, movement shooters, arena shooters, and relevant open-source projects—and study how they solve movement, combat feel, enemy readability, encounter design, arena design, tutorials, progression, visual feedback, UI, bosses, and overall game cohesion. Where appropriate, examine open-source implementations and existing mechanics to understand how proven systems are built rather than reinventing everything poorly. The goal is not to blindly copy another game's appearance, but to understand why successful mechanics, code structures, encounters, arenas, and feedback systems work and adapt those principles into this game's own identity. I want significantly more cohesion across the entire game, and I want us to establish actual design rules and principles that future levels, arenas, enemies, bosses, mechanics, and UI must follow so that we stop forgetting important elements between iterations.
>
> Starting with Level 1, the early pure wall-riding section and its entire arena need to be completely overhauled. Right now it is too difficult in the wrong way, too unforgiving, boring, and easy to get stuck in. The gaps between walls are too wide relative to the player's speed and the timing window is excessively tight, so succeeding feels more like fighting the movement system than mastering it. Redesign this section so that wall riding is satisfying and readable while still requiring skill. Consider adding a meaningful environmental hazard such as lava or another dangerous surface beneath the player so that failure has a clear consequence and the environment itself has more visual and gameplay identity. The player should still be able to dash during these sections, and dashing should be introduced and taught earlier so that players understand it before encounters start demanding it.
>
> Near the end of Level 1 before the first boss, there is another area containing roughly three walls where the player progressively gains height by jumping or bouncing between them. This is also much too difficult because of the player's speed and the extremely tight timing. The problem is not simply that the challenge is difficult; the movement speed itself makes the intended action inconsistent. You move so quickly after leaving one wall that you can fly past the next wall before having a reasonable opportunity to attach to it. Balance the wall spacing, acceleration, wall-jump impulse, attachment forgiveness, input buffering, and any other relevant variables so that the sequence works naturally at the speed the game expects the player to move. Perhaps the walls should be slightly closer together so that even though the player is moving quickly, the rapid bounce from wall to wall naturally carries them upward.
>
> More broadly, the wall-running/wall-riding mechanic itself still does not feel smooth enough. It is clunky and buggy, sometimes snaps too aggressively, sometimes causes the player to fall off awkwardly, and can conflict with other inputs such as pressing B or attempting another movement action. It needs to become smooth, predictable, responsive, and consistent. The player should understand why they attached to or detached from a wall, and the system should include appropriate forgiveness such as input buffering, coyote-time-like behavior where appropriate, better wall detection, and cleaner transitions between wall riding, jumping, dashing, falling, and attacking.
>
> The arena intended to teach wall riding while shooting also needs substantial redesign. Currently the enemies are already awkwardly floating there while the player is being taught the mechanic, even though the player cannot properly engage them yet. Instead, structure the tutorial as an intentional sequence: teach the player to begin wall riding, clearly display the required input, confirm that the player is successfully performing it, and only then spawn or activate the enemies—or introduce them immediately before they become attackable in a way that makes visual and gameplay sense. The world should feel believable according to its own rules rather than having enemies visibly floating around waiting for a tutorial trigger.
>
> There are also bugs where all enemies in an arena can be destroyed but the exit door still fails to open. Fix the encounter-completion logic so this cannot happen, and add a small, unobtrusive UI element showing how many enemies remain in the current locked encounter. It should be tucked into a corner and visually integrated with the rest of the HUD rather than becoming distracting.
>
> More generally, the entire UI needs a major visual overhaul. The health bar, fonts, tutorial prompts, menus, score screens, shop screens, encounter information, and other interface elements currently look too generic and cheap, almost like basic placeholder or default AI-generated UI. Establish a strong visual language for the game with better typography, hierarchy, spacing, colors, animation, effects, and thematic elements. The health bar specifically should look much cooler and more intentional. Tutorials also need to be clearer and more immediately readable. Study how games such as ULTRAKILL present important tutorial text prominently in the center of the screen without permanently obstructing gameplay, and create a similarly effective system appropriate for this game.
>
> The game as a whole also needs stronger lighting, effects, environmental composition, and visual polish. We have a potentially cool neon aesthetic, but currently it often feels soulless because the environments rely on basic geometry with a few neon lights rather than having a strong identity. I do not want us to solve that by returning to meaningless decorative clutter; decorations should support the environment, gameplay, theme, navigation, or worldbuilding. Study how successful movement shooters and boomer shooters create memorable arenas without sacrificing gameplay readability.
>
> The arenas themselves remain one of the weakest parts of the game and need far more intentional design. Every arena should be built around the player's available mechanics, enemy roster, movement possibilities, objectives, combat rhythm, and intended learning or mastery goal. We need to define a reusable set of arena-design principles so future arenas are not simply collections of platforms, ramps, walls, and enemies placed without purpose.
>
> There is a room after the wall-riding encounter where the player kills an enemy while wall riding/holding LT or using slow motion. After completing it, the transition through the door looks ugly, there is a strange pink effect that appears and seemingly should not be there, and the player essentially runs into what feels like a brick wall. That transition needs to be redesigned so movement through the level remains visually and mechanically coherent.
>
> I initially thought the game then repeated the triple-wall section in reverse, which seemed pointless because the player was already at the top and could simply walk downward; I later realized I had accidentally turned around and backtracked. However, that mistake exposes another design problem: once a player completes a room, proceeds into the next room, and the progression door closes behind them, they generally should not be able to accidentally wander backward into completed content unless backtracking is deliberately part of the design. Use progression gating, one-way transitions, clearer environmental direction, or other solutions so players naturally continue forward instead of accidentally retracing completed rooms. The larger point about establishing consistent design rules still stands.
>
> The first boss fight is conceptually very cool, and I like the foundation, but the gameplay currently does not function consistently enough. Wall riding during the fight is unreliable, particularly when repeatedly riding upward and jumping between surfaces. After a certain number of jumps or transitions, the player simply cannot consistently make the movement work. Fix this at the movement-system level rather than designing around a broken mechanic. The boss also needs a clearly readable health or damage-state system. I do not know whether the health bar disappearing is intentional, but the player needs clear feedback explaining whether the boss is taking damage, whether it is vulnerable, what phase it is in, and what the player's attacks are accomplishing. Currently the player can suddenly find themselves shooting pistols and wall riding against this massive boss without enough communication about how the encounter works. The introduction itself is great, but I want it to feel even more cinematic. Improve the entrance, framing, animation, sound, camera presentation, lighting, and pacing; potentially experiment with a very brief in-engine cinematic or controlled reveal if it does not interrupt the game's momentum. Most importantly, however, the gameplay has to work. The boss should have meaningful phases, evolving attacks, and readable transitions. I like that the current attacks force the player to parry, so preserve and build upon that concept, but the encounter currently asks the player to process too much while the underlying systems are still inconsistent. Fix the fundamentals first and then build complexity on top of them.
>
> The Pulse Gauntlet should also attack somewhat faster. Experiment with an attack interval around 1.25 to 1.3 seconds, or otherwise tune it within that general range so it feels faster and more responsive while remaining balanced. The gauntlet's projectile-reflection/parry behavior also needs improvement. Against flying enemies, the player currently has to wait until an incoming projectile is extremely close before the gauntlet can successfully hit or reflect it. This makes the mechanic feel unreliable and unnecessarily dangerous. Investigate whether the problem is the melee hitbox, projectile hitbox, parry detection range, timing window, animation timing, or a combination of these. The player should be able to intentionally intercept a projectile slightly farther away, with enough visual and mechanical forgiveness that a successful parry feels like deliberate timing rather than luck.
>
> When the player gets the gun again and enters the room containing additional enemies, I like the encounter concept. Expand it so it actively encourages combining the gun with LB dashing and the gauntlet. Teach or encourage the player to dash directly into enemies while attacking with the gauntlet. If the player performs a high-speed dash impact or gauntlet attack, create a powerful special feedback response. I imagine a vivid magenta/hot-pink reaction created by combining the game's blues with its orange/red energy palette, almost like an explosive chemical reaction caused by speed and impact. The inspiration in my head is the dramatic visual interaction in Demon Slayer when different effects collide, such as Tanjiro's sword visuals combining with Blood Demon Art. Do not literally copy that effect, but capture the idea of two forces combining into something visually explosive. The impact should be communicated not only through particles but through animation, sound, camera shake, screen effects, HUD response, controller vibration/haptics, hit-stop if appropriate, and other feedback so the player can physically feel the difference between a normal attack and a high-speed empowered impact.
>
> This principle should extend to combat generally. Gunshots and enemy hits currently need substantially better impact feedback. Charged attacks, especially charged shotgun attacks, should produce more dramatic particles, explosions, enemy reactions, sound, camera response, and other feedback. The flamethrower currently feels weak and needs much richer particles, stronger audio/visual feedback, and appropriate controller vibration or haptic feedback so firing it communicates sustained power. Boss hits should also have smoother and crisper animations, and striking a boss during a special vulnerability state with the intended weapon or gauntlet attack should create especially strong feedback through particles, screen effects, camera shake, animation reactions, sound, and potentially short hit-stop or other techniques.
>
> Enemy art direction also needs a significant overhaul while preserving the strongest existing ideas. I like the pill-shaped enemy concept and want that general silhouette language to remain, but the enemies need more life and personality. Give them better-looking visors and use different visor shapes, shield designs, armor details, attachments, silhouettes, animation styles, and small visual elements to communicate enemy class and behavior. They should not simply be perfect pill shapes or collections of geometric primitives. Preserve the orange, red, and yellow palette because it contrasts extremely well with the environment, but develop it into a more sophisticated enemy visual language using shape design, color theory, hierarchy, materials, glow, animation, and recognizable silhouettes. This applies to standard enemies, elite enemies, and bosses. The smaller enemies especially should no longer look like collections of squares and simple geometric shapes. They also currently appear to bug out and sometimes fail to move, so diagnose and fix their AI/navigation/state logic.
>
> The enemy immediately before the Icebreaker fight—the one that feels like a miniature version of the boss—is a great addition. Add a few more encounters with enemies like this before the boss and potentially use them in other arenas so players gradually learn pieces of the boss's language before encountering the full version. Preserve this concept of foreshadowing bosses through smaller enemies, but redesign their visuals along with the rest of the roster so they have recognizable shapes, personality, themes, and coherent color theory instead of being composed primarily from squares and rectangles.
>
> The shop screen, post-level score screen, and other full-screen UI also need substantial redesign. They currently feel like large amounts of plain text placed over the game. Blur or otherwise visually separate the gameplay background more effectively, organize information into concise and attractive interface components, establish stronger hierarchy, use the game's color palette more deliberately, and add tasteful animations and visual details. These screens should feel like part of the same game rather than generic overlays.
>
> Level 3 also needs substantial iteration. The first room is confusing and should be redesigned so its intended movement and combat path are readable. It currently contains a strange ramp and other geometry that feels placed simply because an object needed to be there. Every ramp, wall, platform, obstacle, hazard, and piece of cover should have a gameplay reason. Arena geometry should encourage movement mechanics, create combat choices, manipulate sightlines, support enemy behaviors, establish rhythm, or teach/reward a skill rather than merely filling empty space.
>
> The shotgun-jump or downward-fire jump mechanic also needs work. When I press jump and shoot downward at approximately the same time, I do not receive nearly enough height, and the inputs do not feel properly synchronized. Even when the timing feels correct, the boost can be insufficient to complete the first challenge. Make this mechanic much more responsive and fluid. There should be a reasonable timing window around the jump input and downward shot where the actions combine to produce the intended strong vertical boost. Consider input buffering and other timing forgiveness rather than requiring frame-perfect execution. The tutorial should introduce the mechanic using smaller, easier jumps first and progressively increase the required height or precision after the player understands the interaction.
>
> Level 3 and later levels also need stronger enemy compositions and clearer difficulty progression. Use more flying enemies, including the charging variants, and introduce one or two additional enemy archetypes so arenas increasingly demand that the player combine movement, target prioritization, weapons, parrying, dashing, and positioning. Difficulty should scale not only by increasing enemy health or enemy count but by creating richer combinations of enemy behaviors while the player simultaneously becomes more comfortable and powerful. Research encounter-design principles from successful games and establish formal rules for how new enemies are introduced, reinforced, combined, escalated, and eventually mastered.
>
> I also want worm-like enemies added to appropriate Level 3 arenas. These could burrow underneath the player and emerge aggressively, creating a movement threat that forces the player to react vertically or time an attack. For example, the player might shotgun-jump away as the worm emerges, or deliberately time a shotgun blast, blaster shot, melee strike, or gauntlet attack against it during its emergence window. Successful timing should trigger a satisfying special reaction, animation, or effect. These enemies could potentially absorb substantial shotgun damage depending on balance, but the important thing is that their behavior creates an interesting movement/combat interaction rather than simply functioning as another health pool. Their designs should continue the orange/red/yellow enemy palette while avoiding the same overly geometric square-based aesthetic. Give them recognizable biological/mechanical forms, distinctive movement, readable attack anticipation, and enough personality that players immediately understand that they are a unique enemy class. The worm boss arena itself also needs a major visual redesign so it feels like an intentional boss location rather than another generic geometric room.
>
> More broadly, the game needs significantly more personality and cohesion. The neon aesthetic has potential, but neon strips attached to simple geometry are not enough to create a memorable world. Research comparable games and determine how they create strong environmental identity while maintaining extremely readable high-speed combat. Create an art-direction and arena-design framework that defines what belongs in this world, what does not, how environments communicate progression and navigation, how color is used, how enemy colors contrast against environments, how interactive objects are distinguished from decoration, how hazards are communicated, how arenas accommodate each movement mechanic, and how visual complexity is controlled so combat remains readable.
>
> I am stopping my playthrough feedback around Level 3 because this is already extremely extensive, but I want you to treat this as a challenge to make a major leap in quality rather than a collection of tiny patches. I care much more about cohesion, intentionality, responsiveness, and polish across the entire experience. I want you to spend substantial time researching and implementing this properly. This can be a long autonomous development session—potentially four to five hours if the environment allows it—and I want significant actual building and iteration accomplished during that time. Do not spend the majority of the session repeatedly testing tiny changes without making meaningful progress. Testing and live feedback are obviously necessary, but balance them against implementation: build substantial improvements, test them intelligently, iterate based on what you learn, and continue building. Prioritize foundational systems first where they affect many other problems—for example, fixing wall-running consistency before trying to tune every wall-running encounter individually—then redesign encounters and arenas around the improved systems. Establish documented principles for movement, combat feedback, enemy design, arena design, tutorials, progression, UI, art direction, and boss encounters so future work follows the same standards. When you encounter an unclear design problem, research proven solutions and reason from the game's core goals rather than choosing the easiest implementation. The final result of this iteration should feel substantially more responsive, intentional, visually cohesive, readable, satisfying, and alive, with meaningful progress across movement, combat, levels, enemies, bosses, UI, effects, and overall game identity rather than isolated superficial changes.
